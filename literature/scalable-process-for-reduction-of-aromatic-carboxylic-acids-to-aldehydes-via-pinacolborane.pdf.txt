ABSTRACT: The transformation of carboxylic acids to their corresponding aldehydes is one of the most important reactions in
both organic syntheses and industrial applications. Our previous research reported an efficient and convenient aldehyde preparative
method via controllable reduction of carboxylic acids with pinacolborane (HBpin/DMAP). However, the new method still suffered
from some limitations during scaling-up, for example, a large excess of reagents, obvious exotherms, and silica gel column
purification. The process development was performed to address these troublesome issues and also to make the new method a
practical synthetic protocol. After the detailed exploration of reaction parameters, including the reaction conditions, charging mode,
and workup methods, a safe, efficient, and column-free process of reduction of aromatic carboxylic acids to aldehydes via DMAP/
HBpin has been successfully established. The newly developed robust process could provide a practical and alternative method for
straightforward access of aldehydes from carboxylic acids.
KEYWORDS: process development, reduction of carboxylic acid, aldehyde, pinacolborane (HBpin), scaling-up

1. INTRODUCTION

for CAs

Aldehydes are extremely important structural motifs as well as
crucial starting materials and/or intermediates in the field of
synthetic chemistry.1 Reduction of carboxylic acids (CAs) to
aldehydes is one of the most fundamental organic reactions.
Over the past century, quite a few methods have been
developed for the reduction of CAs and their derivatives to
aldehydes.2 Compared to the starting CAs, the intrinsically
higher reactivity of the resulting aldehydes would pose a
challenge to chemoselectivity; i.e., direct reduction of CAs
would form aldehydes and/or alcohols. Indeed, most of the
reported methods
to aldehydes were indirect
approaches or multiple-step reductions.2,3 Recently, we have
reported a direct and controllable reduction method, which
could readily and efficiently convert CAs to their correspond-
room temperature.4,5 The new method
ing aldehydes at
applied HBpin as the reductant together with 4-(piperidin-1-
yl)-N-trifluoromethylsulfonylpyridin-N-ium trifluoromethane-
sulfonate (Tf-PPDP) as the activator (Scheme 1). Previously,
boranes have been extensively applied for the full reduction of
CAs and aldehydes to alcohols in both laboratory and
industrial scales;6−8 thus, our HBpin reduction exhibited the
unique value of this controlled reduction of CAs to aldehydes.
The newly reported method features easy operation and
exhibits high potential for applications in laboratory synthesis.
However, upon scaling up the reaction, some problematic
issues were encountered that needed to be addressed. (1)
Excess reagents were used. For example, 1.7 equiv of Tf-PPDP,
1.6 equiv of PPDP, and 1.1 equiv of HBpin were necessary for
high conversion and yield, and among them, PPDP was quite
expensive. (2) Very fast reaction accompanied by a noticeable
exotherm was observed during the reaction. The risk of
uncontrollable reaction temperature and even occasionally

spraying of the reaction mixture exists. (3) Poor mixing caused
by single feeding could lead to the formation of byproducts
and would further result in repeatability. (4) The silica gel
column purification was not favorable during scaling-up due to
concerns about efficiency or effluent disposal. To solve these
issues, we have developed a robust process of direct reduction
of CAs to aldehydes,
featuring safety, controllability, high
selectivity, reproducibility, and simple purification suitable for
potential scale-up applications.

2.2. Solvent. Previous research used dichloromethane
(DCM) as the solvent for its better solubility of Tf-PPDP.
Unfortunately, Tf-DMAP exhibits limited solubility in DCM.
Moreover, the relatively low boiling point of DCM (∼40 °C)
is not a proper choice for scaling-up due to both the potential
exothermic nature of the reaction and its narrow operational
temperature range. Thus, various solvents were initially
screened to evaluate the solubility of the substrate and reagent,
as well as the effects on the reactivity.

According to the mechanism, 1.0 equiv of Tf-DMAP and 1.0
equiv of DMAP were theoretically required for the formation
of acyl-DMAP in step 1. The reduction step required 1.0 equiv
of DMAP and 1.0 equiv of HBpin, while excess HBpin would
result in an over-reduction to yield the alcohol byproduct. Of
the above considerations, 1.2 equiv of Tf-DMAP (slightly
excess), 2.1 equiv of DMAP (slightly excess), and 0.9 equiv of
HBpin (slightly shortage) were employed during the following
optimization.

The process was initiatively split into two stages and we
abandoned the previous one-pot operation mode. Tf-DMAP,
DMAP, and solvent were added into the reaction flask and

stirred at room temperature for 3 min. To the resulting mixture
was added 2-naphthoic acid,
followed by stirring for an
additional 3 min. Solvent test experiments were carried out in
1,2-dichloroethane (DCE), tetrahydrofuran (THF), toluene,
acetonitrile (MeCN), 1,4-dioxane, and dimethoxyethane
(DME), the results of which are shown in Figure 1. The
pictures clearly reflect that only MeCN as a solvent could form
a clear solution.

When all slurries were heated to 60 °C, the mixture in DCE
turned into a clear solution, while others remained as
suspensions. After keeping for 3 h at 60 °C, all reaction
mixtures were cooled to room temperature, and then HBpin
was added and stirred for 0.5 h. Quantitative analyses via gas
chromatography (GC) were carried out with biphenyl as the
internal standard. The results are summarized in Table 1. As
can be seen, with MeCN as the solvent, the reaction mixture
always remained a clear solution, and the preliminary results
showed the highest yield (entry 4, Table 1). Therefore, MeCN
was chosen for further optimization.

3. CONCLUSION

Based on the innovative chemistry of Tf-DMAP-mediated
HBpin reduction of carboxylic acid, we have successfully
developed a robust reduction process of aromatic carboxylic

acid to its corresponding aldehyde. The new process featured
the advantages of safety, cost-effectiveness, “greenness”, being
column-free, and high efficiency. The new process employed
less expensive and more readily available DMAP to replace the
relatively expensive PPDP (cost). The current work intends for
rapid conversion of carboxylic acid to aldehyde on a
subkilogram scale. Safety and toxicity concerns have not
been encountered, and the reagent could be operated safely
industrial-scale

4. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION

4.1. General. All 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded
on a Bruker DRX 500 MHz spectrometer in deuterium solvent
with tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard. NMR
multiplicities are abbreviated as follows: s = singlet, d =
doublet, t = triplet, q = quartet, sept = septet, m = multiplet,
and br = broad signal. Chemical shifts are given in parts per
million and are referenced to TMS (1H and 13C). GC analysis
was performed on an Agilent 7890A instrument with a flame
ionization detector (FID). An HP-5 capillary column (30 m,
0.32 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness) was used. GC−MS
analysis was performed on a Thermo Trace ISQ instrument
with a xx detector. A TG 5MS capillary column (60 m, 0.25
mm i.d., and 0.25 μm film thickness) was used. All chemicals
were commercially available and used as received.

4.2. Synthesis of Tf-DMAP. To a 2000 mL three-necked
jacketed flask with a mechanic stirrer were added 122.2 g of
DMAP (1.0 mol) and 1.6 L of dichloromethane. The mixture
was stirred until the solid dissolved completely. The temper-
ature of the mixture was cooled to 0 °C. 352.7 g of Tf2O
(352.7 g, 1.2 mol, 1 equiv) was added slowly into the flask over
1.5 h. Then, the circulating water bath was removed. The
temperature of the mixture was gradually raised to room
temperature and stirred for additional 4 h. After filtration,
elution with dichloromethane, and drying under vacuum, 375.7
g of Tf-DMAP was obtained as a white solid (yield: 93%). 1H
NMR (500 MHz, CD3CN) δ 8.29 (d, J = 8.0 Hz, 2H), 7.16 (d,
J = 8.0 Hz, 2H), 3.41 (s, 6H) ppm.

4.3. Typical Procedure for the Reduction of Aromatic
Carboxylic Acid to Aldehyde. To a 1000 mL four-necked
jacketed flask with a mechanic stirrer were added 25.8 g of 2-
naphthoic acid (0.15 mol), 22.0 g of DMAP (0.18 mol, 1.2
equiv), and 120 mL of acetonitrile. The mixture was stirred for
3 min and heated to 60 °C. A solution of Tf-DMAP (72.8 g,
0.18 mol, 1.2 equiv) in 180 mL of acetonitrile was prepared
separately and added dropwise to the jacketed flask over 1 h.
The mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 3 h, then cooled to room
temperature and subsequently to 0 °C using a circulating cold
bath. Meanwhile, to another 250 mL round-bottom flask were
added 18.3 g of DMAP (0.15 mol, 1.0 equiv), 100 mL of
acetonitrile, and 19.2 g of HBpin (0.15 mol, 1.0 equiv),
affording a clear solution after stirring for 3 min. The solution
of HBpin-DMAP was added dropwise to the cooled reaction
mixture (0 °C) over 40 min. After being stirred for 30 min, the
mixture was quenched with 80 mL of saturated aq MgSO4. The
solvent was removed under reduced pressure. 300 mL of n-
heptane was added to the aqueous phase, and the biphasic
mixture was heated to 60 °C. The aqueous phase was adjusted
to neutral pH with aq HCl (2 mol/L). Then, the mixture was
stirred at 60 °C for 1 h, and the aqueous phase was separated.

The extraction with n-heptane was repeated twice more, and all
of the organic phase was collected. After drying with Na2SO4,
filtration, and concentration, 21.5 g of 2-naphthaldehyde was
obtained as a white solid (yield: 92%). 1H NMR (500 MHz,
CDCl3) δ 10.16 (s, 1H), 8.33 (s, 1H), 8.03−7.87 (m, 4H),
7.68−7.63 (m, 1H), 7.62−7.57 (m, 1H) ppm.
